# Systems of units

A system of units is a consistent set of units built from a few base units.

## The International System (SI)

It is the official system of almost every country, and the reference for science, industry and trade. It is maintained by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM), in Sèvres, France.

Its seven base units:

| Unit | Symbol | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| meter | m | length |
| kilogram | kg | mass |
| second | s | time |
| ampere | A | electric current |
| kelvin | K | temperature |
| mole | mol | amount of substance |
| candela | cd | luminous intensity |

The other SI units derive from these and are **coherent**: no numerical factor appears in their definitions. The newton is exactly 1 kg·m/s², the joule 1 N·m, the watt 1 J/s.

### The 2019 redefinition

Since 20 May 2019, the seven base units no longer rely on particular objects or experiments, but on seven **constants of nature** whose values were fixed once and for all:

- the second, on the frequency of a transition of the caesium-133 atom;
- the meter, on the speed of light (exactly 299,792,458 m/s);
- the kilogram, on the Planck constant. It replaced the platinum-iridium cylinder kept in Sèvres since 1889, whose mass drifted by a few micrograms;
- the ampere, on the charge of the electron;
- the kelvin, on the Boltzmann constant;
- the mole, on the Avogadro number;
- the candela, on the luminous efficacy of 540 THz green light.

As a result, units can now be reproduced anywhere with the same accuracy, without any physical standard.

## Non-SI units accepted for use

Some units are not part of the SI but are accepted for use with it, because they are well established: the liter (1 dm³), the tonne (1,000 kg), the hectare (10,000 m²), the minute, hour and day, the degree of angle, the degree Celsius (whose step equals one kelvin), the electronvolt, the astronomical unit.

## Imperial and US customary units

They come from the old English measures. They are often all called "imperial", but there are really **two families**, which differ for volumes and large masses:

- the British **imperial units**, set in 1824;
- the **US customary units**, derived from English measures before 1824.

Since the 1959 international agreement, both families share the same inch (exactly 2.54 cm) and the same pound (exactly 0.453 592 37 kg): they are therefore defined from the SI.

| Unit | United Kingdom | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Gallon | 4.546 L | 3.785 L |
| Pint | 568 mL | 473 mL |
| Fluid ounce | 28.4 mL | 29.6 mL |
| Ton | long ton: 1,016 kg | short ton: 907 kg |
| Inch, foot, yard, mile | identical | identical |
| Pound, ounce (mass) | identical | identical |

Today, only the United States mainly uses its customary units. The United Kingdom is officially metric, but keeps the mile on roads, the pint in pubs and the stone for body weight.

## Other systems

- **The CGS system** (centimeter, gram, second): an older metric system, still found in theoretical physics and astronomy (dyne, erg, gauss).
- **Navigation units**: the nautical mile (1,852 m) and the knot (one nautical mile per hour), tied to the geography of the globe.
- **Natural units**: in particle physics, constants such as the speed of light are set to 1, which simplifies equations.

Large astronomical distances (astronomical unit, light-year, parsec) are non-SI **units** of length, not systems of their own.
