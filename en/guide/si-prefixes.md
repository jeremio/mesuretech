# SI prefixes

A prefix multiplies a unit by a power of 10: a kilometer is 1,000 meters, a milligram one thousandth of a gram. The same prefixes apply to every SI unit.

Ronna, ronto, quetta and quecto were added in 2022.

::: warning A billion is not a "billion"
In English, *a billion* is 10⁹. In French and most other European languages, *un billion* is 10¹² and 10⁹ is *un milliard*. Prefixes, however, are the same in every language: giga = 10⁹ everywhere.
:::

## Writing rules

- The prefix symbol is attached to the unit symbol, with no space: km, mg, µs.
- Case matters: **m** (milli, 10⁻³) versus **M** (mega, 10⁶); **k** (kilo) is lowercase.
- Prefixes are never stacked: write nanometer (nm), not millimicrometer.
- The kilogram is the only base unit that already contains a prefix: multiples of mass are built on the gram (milligram, not microkilogram).

**Examples:** 1 km = 1,000 m; 1 mg = 0.001 g; 1 GHz = 1,000,000,000 Hz; 1 µs = 0.000 001 s; 1 mA = 0.001 A.

## Binary prefixes (computing)

Computer memory naturally comes in powers of 2. For years, "kilo" meant 1,024 rather than 1,000 there, which still causes confusion. Since 1998, the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) has defined separate binary prefixes:

| Binary prefix | Symbol | Value | Close to |
|---|---|---|---|
| kibi | Ki | 2¹⁰ = 1,024 | kilo (10³) |
| mebi | Mi | 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 | mega (10⁶) |
| gibi | Gi | 2³⁰ = 1,073,741,824 | giga (10⁹) |
| tebi | Ti | 2⁴⁰ = 1,099,511,627,776 | tera (10¹²) |
| pebi | Pi | 2⁵⁰ | peta (10¹⁵) |

The gap grows with size: 2.4% between a KiB and a kB, 10% between a TiB and a TB. That is why a drive sold as "1 TB" (10¹² bytes) shows up as "931 GB" in Windows, which actually counts in GiB. The [data converter](/en/converters/data) offers both conventions.
